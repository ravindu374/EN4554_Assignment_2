4. Task 3 — Regularization and shortcuts

What the assignment asked (a) Train a small CNN with batch norm on 5k training images. Compare: baseline, weight decay, dropout, flip+crop, cutout, mixup, and all combined (implement cutout and mixup yourself). Tabulate train accuracy, validation accuracy, and their gap; plot the curves.
(b) Build CIFAR-style plain and residual networks of depth $6n+2$ for depths 8, 20 and 56 (three stages, channels 16/32/64, global average pooling, batch norm, Kaiming initialisation). The only difference between the two families is the shortcut. Plot the training loss versus epoch and explain.
The crucial framing: these are two different failures Part (a) is about too little data — the network memorises the training set and learns nothing general. Part (b) is about too many layers — the network cannot be trained at all, even on data it is actively being fitted to. They look similar from a distance ("the deep model is worse") but they have completely different causes and completely different cures. Being able to tell them apart is the point of this task.

4.1 Part (a) — six ways to stop a network memorising

The training set is deliberately cut to 5,000 images, one ninth of the full split. Regularization only matters when data is scarce: with all 45,000 images a 141k-parameter CNN barely overfits, and the effect would be too small to measure.

What each method does

MethodMathematicsIn plain words
weight decay$L' = L + \lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$,  $\lambda = 5\times10^{-4}$
i.e. $g \leftarrow g + \lambda w$		 Adds a penalty for being large, pulling every weight gently towards zero. Simpler functions, less room to memorise.
dropout$p = 0.3$ Randomly switches off 30% of the features during training. The network cannot rely on any one feature always being present.
flip + crop— Randomly mirror the image left-right, and take a random 32×32 window from a 4-pixel-padded version. The network never sees the same framing twice.
cutoutzero an $8\times8$ square Blanks out a random patch. If the net leans on one distinctive feature — a cat's ear — it fails whenever that feature is hidden, so it is forced to use the whole picture.
mixupsee below Trains on blends of two images with blended labels. Forces sensible behaviour in the gaps between training examples.

The two I implemented from scratch

Cutout picks a random centre and zeroes an $8\times8$ square. The zeroing happens after normalisation, so the hole takes the per-channel mean colour rather than black.

Mixup blends two images and their labels in the same proportion:

$$\tilde x = \lambda x_i + (1-\lambda)x_j \qquad L = \lambda\,\mathrm{CE}\big(f(\tilde x), y_i\big) + (1-\lambda)\,\mathrm{CE}\big(f(\tilde x), y_j\big)$$

with $\lambda \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)$ and $\alpha = 1$ (which makes $\lambda$ uniform on $[0,1]$).

Why mixup is not as strange as it looks Asking a network to predict "60% cat, 40% ship" sounds absurd. The purpose is to force the network to behave linearly between training examples. Left alone, a network makes wildly overconfident predictions in the empty space between the examples it has seen — and that over-confidence is the memorisation. Mixup fills that space with sensible targets.
A measurement decision that matters Training accuracy is always measured on clean, un-augmented images, in a separate pass. If it were measured on augmented batches, mixup's "training accuracy" would be computed against blended labels — a different quantity entirely — and the gap column would not be comparable across rows. The gap is the whole point of the table, so it has to measure the same thing everywhere.

The clearest finding: only augmentation actually stops memorisation Look at the training-accuracy column. Baseline, weight decay and dropout all reach exactly 100% — they memorise all 5,000 images perfectly despite the regularization. Only the methods that change the data (flip+crop, and the combination) prevent that, because the network never sees the same image twice and so has nothing fixed to memorise.

So the two mechanisms are not equally effective here: constraining the weights barely helped; changing the data did.
Why dropout did almost nothing — worth being able to explain In my SmallCNN, dropout sits only after global average pooling, on a 128-dimensional vector just before the classifier. That is a much weaker intervention than dropout between convolutional layers. The result is a property of where it was placed, not evidence that dropout is useless.

Similarly, weight decay is applied to every parameter including the batch-norm scale and shift. Common practice excludes those. Both choices are defensible, but they explain the small effects.
Two results that look like failures but are findings Mixup hurt (−1.9 points). With $\alpha=1$ on only 5,000 images, mixup is very strong — nearly every training image is a blend, and the network gets little clean signal. Mixup usually needs more data or longer training to pay off.

"All combined" is not the best. It has by far the smallest gap (7.5 points) but only 68.82% validation — below flip+crop alone at 70.88%. Five regularizers on 5,000 images over-constrain a small network: training accuracy falls to 72.56%, which is underfitting, not successful regularization. More regularization is not automatically better.
The 30-second version of part (a) "With only 5,000 images my network memorised them perfectly — 100% on training, 67% on validation. Penalising the weights barely helped; it still memorised. What worked was changing the pictures: flipping and cropping them so it never saw the same image twice. That gave the best result. And stacking every technique at once actually made it worse — it closed the gap, but by making the network too constrained to learn."

4.2 Part (b) — the degradation problem

These are the CIFAR networks from the ResNet paper: one stem convolution, then three stages of $n$ blocks each at 16, 32 and 64 channels, then global average pooling and a linear classifier. Each block has two $3\times3$ convolutions, so total depth is

$$\text{depth} = 6n + 2 \qquad n=1 \Rightarrow 8,\quad n=3 \Rightarrow 20,\quad n=9 \Rightarrow 56$$

The only difference between the two families

PLAIN BLOCK RESIDUAL BLOCK x x ---------------+ | | | conv 3x3 conv 3x3 | shortcut batchnorm batchnorm | (identity) ReLU ReLU | | | | conv 3x3 conv 3x3 | batchnorm batchnorm | | | | | + <--------------+ | | ReLU ReLU | | out out out = F(x) out = F(x) + x

In code it is literally one line:

if self.residual:
    out = out + self.shortcut(x)
Keeping the comparison honest: Option A shortcuts Where a block changes shape, the shortcut has to match. I used Option A from the ResNet paper — subsample with a stride and zero-pad the extra channels — rather than a $1\times1$ convolution. Option A has no parameters at all, so:

Also: no data augmentation in part (b) Unlike part (a). The question here is whether a deep network can be optimised, so the networks are allowed to fit the training data as hard as they can, and the training-loss curves are compared directly. Augmentation would damp exactly the effect being measured.

The headline numbers
  • plain-56 trains 200× worse than plain-20 (0.7719 vs 0.0037) despite having three times more parameters.
  • residual-56 trains 860× better than plain-56 (0.0009 vs 0.7719) with exactly the same parameter count.
  • At depth 8 the two are effectively tied (0.0617 vs 0.0641).
Why this is NOT overfitting — the single most important point Overfitting means low training loss with poor validation accuracy. Here the training loss itself is bad — on data the network is actively being fitted to. That is a different disease entirely.

And it should be impossible. A 56-layer network can represent anything a 20-layer one can: just set the extra 36 layers to the identity function and you recover the shallower network exactly. So plain-56 has strictly more capacity and must be able to reach at least as low a training loss as plain-20.

It fails to. So the capacity is there and gradient descent cannot find it. This is an optimisation failure, and the ResNet paper named it the degradation problem.
Why the shortcut fixes it A residual block computes $\text{out} = F(x) + x$. To represent the identity it only needs to drive $F$ to zero — and driving a thing to zero is the easiest thing a network with weight decay does. A plain block must learn to reproduce its own input before it can improve on it, and 56 layers of that is hard.

The shortcut also gives the gradient a path with derivative exactly 1 all the way back to earlier layers — the clean route that Task 1(d) showed sigmoid lacked. ReLU and Kaiming initialisation fixed the signal at initialisation; at 56 layers it degrades again during training, and the shortcut is what holds the path open.
Reading the depth-8 tie correctly At 8 layers there is no degradation to fix, so the shortcut buys nothing — plain-8 even edges ahead by a hair (0.0617 vs 0.0641). This is a feature of the result, not noise to explain away: it shows the benefit is specifically about depth, not a free improvement you get everywhere.

Also note residual-56 (0.0009) beat residual-20 (0.0017) on training loss but not on validation accuracy (80.78% vs 83.16%). With only 30 epochs and no augmentation, the 56-layer network fits the training set hardest and generalises slightly worse. The ResNet paper trained for 180 epochs with augmentation.
The 30-second version of part (b) "I built the same network twice, plain and with shortcut connections, with identical parameter counts. At 8 and 20 layers they tie. At 56 layers the plain one breaks — its training loss is 200 times worse than its own 20-layer version, even though it has three times more parameters. That is not overfitting, because the training loss is what got worse. The deeper network could copy the shallower one by setting layers to the identity, so the ability is there and gradient descent just cannot find it. Adding one line — out = out + x — fixes it completely: the residual version reaches 0.0009 instead of 0.77."
